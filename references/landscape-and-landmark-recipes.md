# Landscape and Landmark Recipes

Read this reference for natural views, city panoramas, monuments, historic buildings, scenic areas, and tourist attractions.

## Landscape sketch

The goal is an observed memory of a place, not a realistic vista. Preserve the dominant landform, weather, path or water direction, and one scale cue. Reduce the rest to colored-pencil masses.

Use exactly three depth bands:

1. **Foreground interruption:** one cropped branch, rock, flower cluster, railing, or traveler fragment.
2. **Middle route:** a path, river, shoreline, field edge, or village band that leads the eye imperfectly.
3. **Background mass:** mountain, forest, sea, cloud, or skyline simplified into one or two large patches.

Loose landscape errors should be spatial rather than noisy:

- the horizon or shoreline changes angle once;
- mountain ridges do not line up perfectly behind each other;
- a path widens too quickly or stops before reaching its destination;
- one foreground object is oversized;
- some color patches ignore the ink boundary or leave paper gaps;
- repeated trees, waves, or rocks collapse into abbreviated marks;
- one marginal note or arrow nearly collides with the view.

### Landscape surface and route budget

- Treat the three depth bands as spatial roles, not three fully filled zones. Use no more than two medium-to-strong colored-pencil masses; let the remaining band read through contour, a weak broken patch, and raw paper.
- Keep roughly one-third to one-half of every broad lake, hill, field, sky, or path mass as irregular exposed paper. Raw paper must work inside the view as water glare, air, distance, dry ground, or light—not survive only around the outer border.
- Draw the main path, shoreline, river edge, and horizon as mostly continuous confident contours. One localized restart or short correction is useful; periodic dots, dashes, beads, or hash marks along a major spatial edge fail the style.
- Do not texture both sides of a route equally. Two incomplete directional pencil patches can establish a whole path or water body.
- Let a few representative groups imply repeated nature: several directional water marks, a few vegetation clusters, or a small cropped leaf group. Never count and render every wave, tree, grass blade, rock, or leaf.
- Distribute at least four different spatial or material error families across safe zones—for example route widening, ridge mismatch, scale exaggeration, fill miss, foreground crop, and one local correction. Do not replace these with global wobble.
- Use only one clear scale cue unless the user requests a populated scene. A traveler, railing fragment, bridge, or house is enough.

```text
Landscape mode: compress [VIEW] into three hand-judged depth bands on warm notebook paper. Preserve [DOMINANT LANDFORM], [WEATHER/LIGHT], [PATH OR WATER DIRECTION], and [ONE SCALE CUE]. Draw the main route, shoreline, and horizon with mostly continuous confident ink contours, allowing only one localized restart or correction. Let the route or ridge drift once, crop one foreground element, and simplify repeated natural detail into a few representative groups. Use no more than two medium-to-strong colored-pencil masses; keep one-third to one-half of each broad natural mass as irregular raw-paper light, air, water glare, or dry ground. The result should contain route widening, ridge disagreement, scale slip, fill miss, and crop errors without periodic dashes, global jitter, realistic gradients, or a texture carpet.
```

Avoid panoramic photorealism, cinematic concept art, complete tree-by-tree rendering, smooth skies, perfectly layered mountains, uniform wave symbols, or a centered postcard composition.

## Landmark study

Separate landmark identity from supporting context before introducing errors.

### Identity lock

Keep these clean enough to read:

- overall hero silhouette;
- roofline, dome, tower, arch, facade rhythm, or other diagnostic geometry;
- primary orientation and viewpoint;
- 2–4 signature structural features;
- user-required signage or colors.

### Safe error zones

Apply looseness mainly to:

- secondary windows, railings, stairs, tiles, trees, crowds, clouds, paving, fences, or foreground props;
- repeated motifs whose exact count is not identity-critical;
- cast shadows, color fills, marginal arrows, and page spacing;
- minor perspective planes around the hero silhouette.

Before adding wobble, delete or merge roughly half of repeated secondary geometry. A few broken marks should stand in for long runs of tiles, balusters, paving joints, windows, or steps. If every repeated element is counted and rendered, the result has already become too architectural for this style.

### Landmark finish and error budget

- Build an **identity skeleton** first: one continuous outer silhouette, the correct count of identity-critical tiers or masses, the primary orientation, the required colors, and only 2–4 signature features. Never use looseness to change this skeleton.
- For each major roof, dome, tower, arch, or facade tier, keep one dominant contour and only the few inner marks needed to explain its structure. Do not trace every rim, cornice, eave, beam, tile, decorative band, or shadow edge.
- Omit 60–80% of repeated secondary geometry. Represent a complete stair, railing, window, tile, baluster, fence, or crowd run with 2–5 irregular diagnostic marks and an unfinished edge.
- Distribute visible error across different causes: one secondary ellipse or roof plane slips, one stair or railing direction disagrees, one repeated row ends early, one pencil patch misses the ink, and one long contour receives a short local correction. Do not repeat the same defect on every tier.
- Keep the hero slightly off-center and use one cropped foreground interruption or tiny visitor as an intentionally awkward counterweight. Preserve raw paper through terraces, sky, paving, and facade bands rather than shading all architectural surfaces.
- Prefer bare handwritten title, underline, and one short arrow note. Avoid surrounding every note with banners or speech bubbles unless the user requests that page language.
- Simplify visitors to a head shape, torso block, and a few limb strokes. They are scale cues, not miniature character illustrations.

```text
Landmark mode: preserve the attraction's unmistakable identity skeleton—one outer silhouette, the correct count of identity-critical tiers or masses, primary orientation, required colors, and [2–4 DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES]. Give every major tier only one dominant contour and a few necessary inner marks. Omit 60–80% of repeated windows, tiles, stairs, rails, balusters, paving, and decoration; let incomplete runs stand for the whole. Put different errors in different safe zones: one secondary plane slips, one stair or railing direction disagrees, one repeated row ends early, one fill misses the ink, and one contour receives a short local correction. Keep the hero off-center with one cropped foreground interruption or diagrammatic visitor. Use bare title, underline, and at most one short arrow note by default. The result is a personal field sketch, not a precise architectural rendering, symmetrical postcard, or fully described monument.
```

If the attraction becomes ambiguous, restore the hero silhouette and diagnostic features before reducing spontaneity elsewhere.

If the attraction looks like a polished postcard or architectural illustration, preserve the hero silhouette but remove secondary detail, open more raw paper, weaken local color coverage, and simplify the visitor and vegetation before adding any new marks.

## Shared composition rule

Natural landscape may occupy 55–75% of the page spatially, but only about 30–55% of the canvas should receive medium-to-strong colored-pencil coverage. Raw paper must remain active inside the landscape, not just outside it. A landmark study should spend 45–65% on the hero structure and keep more blank paper for annotations. Neither mode should use a symmetrical postcard border.
